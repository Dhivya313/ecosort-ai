# Glass Waste

## Examples
- Beverage bottles (beer, wine, soda, water)
- Food jars (pasta sauce, jam, pickles)
- Glass cosmetic containers
- Clear, green, and brown/amber glass containers
- Glass vases and drinkware

## Avoid
- Mixing glass with ceramics, Pyrex, or tempered glass — they have different melting points and contaminate the batch
- Including mirrors or window glass in container-glass recycling
- Recycling broken glass without checking local policy — some programs exclude it for safety
- Placing light bulbs (especially CFL or LED) in glass recycling — they contain different materials and may have hazardous components
- Leaving metal lids on — remove caps and recycle them separately as metal

## Recommended Disposal
1. **Rinse glass containers** to remove food residue; labels can stay on.
2. **Sort by color** (clear, green, brown) if your local facility requests it.
3. **Remove metal lids and caps** — recycle those with metals.
4. **Broken glass**: Wrap in newspaper or place in a labeled rigid container ("BROKEN GLASS") and check if your facility accepts it; otherwise dispose as general waste.
5. **Pyrex, ceramics, and mirrors**: Dispose in general waste or seek specialized recycling.

## Environmental Impact
- Glass is **100 % recyclable** and can be recycled endlessly without loss in quality.
- Recycling glass reduces related air pollution by **20 %** and water pollution by **50 %** compared to making new glass.
- Using **10 % cullet** (recycled glass) in production reduces energy consumption by roughly **2–3 %** — a full batch of cullet saves up to **30 %** energy.
- A glass bottle can take **1 million years** to decompose in a landfill, but can be recycled into a new bottle in as little as **30 days**.

## Eco Tip
Choose products in glass packaging over plastic when possible — glass is infinitely recyclable and does not leach chemicals into food. Save and repurpose glass jars for food storage, candle holders, or organizing small items around the home.
